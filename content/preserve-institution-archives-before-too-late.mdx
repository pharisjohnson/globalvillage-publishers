# How to Preserve Your Institution's Archives Before It's Too Late

Every institution has a story. The question is: is anyone keeping it?

We have worked with dozens of Kenyan schools, companies, and organisations over the years, and one pattern repeats itself with alarming frequency. Someone calls us, excited about creating a commemorative book for an upcoming milestone. And then the panic sets in.

"We know the photos are somewhere..."

"The old principal kept everything in his office, but he retired..."

"We had a filing cabinet full of documents, but it got lost during the renovation..."

The truth is, most institutions do not realise they have an archival problem until they need their archives most — when a centenary, an anniversary, or a major milestone approaches and suddenly there is nothing coherent to work with.

This guide is designed to prevent that panic. Whether you are planning a commemorative book in the next year or the next decade, these steps will help you preserve what matters before it is too late.

## Why archives disappear faster than you think

Archives do not vanish in dramatic ways. They erode. Slowly, quietly, year after year.

A photograph fades in the sun. A document gets misfiled during an office move. A retiring staff member takes their institutional knowledge with them — not out of malice, but because no one asked them to write it down. A hard drive fails and the backup strategy was "we will figure it out later."

In the Kenyan context, there are additional pressures. Physical documents face humidity, dust, and insects. Offices change hands. Schools undergo changes in administration where institutional memory is not always passed down. Power surges can fry electronics. And with limited budgets, "organise the archives" rarely competes with "fix the roof" or "pay the salaries."

The result? By the time most institutions reach a 50th or 100th anniversary, they have lost more than half of their historical material. The stories are still there in people's memories and in scattered documents, but they are fragmented and fragile.

## Step 1: Do an archival audit

Start by asking one simple question: what do we have, and where is it?

Walk through your institution and identify every location where historical material might be stored. This includes:

- Filing cabinets and storage rooms
- Staff offices (especially employees who have been with the institution for many years)
- The school library or resource centre
- The principal's or CEO's office
- External storage
- Digital locations — shared drives, cloud storage, Google Photos, old websites
- Individual staff members' personal collections

Create a simple inventory spreadsheet. For each item or collection, note what it is, where it is located, its approximate date range, and its condition. You do not need to catalogue every single document at this stage. You just need to know what is out there.

## Step 2: What to keep (and what to let go)

Not everything needs to be preserved. If you are facing a mountain of material, prioritise:

### Keep without question

- **Photographs**: Especially those showing people, events, buildings, and daily life. Old photos are irreplaceable.
- **Official documents**: Founding charters, registration certificates, board minutes, strategic plans.
- **Correspondence**: Letters from notable figures, communications about major decisions.
- **Publications**: Old newsletters, magazines, brochures, event programmes.
- **Audiovisual material**: Recordings of speeches, interviews, events.
- **Memorabilia**: Trophies, uniforms, plaques, invitation cards.

### Keep if you have space

- Student or employee records (within privacy guidelines)
- Routine administrative documents
- Newspaper clippings mentioning the institution
- Unofficial photographs and candid shots

### Let go (responsibly)

- Duplicates (keep the best copy)
- Routine financial records past their legal retention period
- Damaged items where content is already preserved elsewhere

## Step 3: Organise what you have

Once you know what exists, it is time to bring order to the chaos.

### Physical organisation

Group materials by type (photos, documents, publications) and then chronologically. Use archival-quality boxes and folders — regular cardboard boxes contain acids that damage paper over time. Label everything clearly with the institution's name, the contents, and date ranges.

Store materials in a cool, dry, dark place. Avoid basements (humidity) and attics (heat). A dedicated filing cabinet in a room with stable temperature and humidity is ideal. If that is not possible, at least keep materials off the floor and away from windows.

### Digital organisation

Create a simple folder structure on a shared drive or cloud storage:

```
/Institution Archives
  /Photographs
    /1950s
    /1960s
    /1970s
    ...
  /Documents
    /Board Minutes
    /Annual Reports
    /Correspondence
    ...
  /Publications
  /Audiovisual
```

Name files consistently. A good format: `YYYY-MM-DD_Description_Location.jpg`. For example: `1972-03-15_FoundationStoneCeremony_AdminBlock.jpg`.

## Step 4: Digitise before it is too late

Digitisation is your insurance policy. Even if you keep the physical originals, having digital copies means the content survives if the physical copy is damaged or lost.

### Photos

A flatbed scanner works for individual photos. For large volumes, consider a document feeder scanner. Scan at 300 DPI minimum (600 DPI for small photos you might want to enlarge later). Save as TIFF for archival quality and JPEG for everyday use.

### Documents

For standard documents, a scanner or a scanning app on a phone works well. Adobe Scan, Google Drive's scan feature, or even a clean photo with good lighting can produce readable digital copies.

For bound volumes (old yearbooks, magazines), you will need a book scanner or a professional service. This is where a publishing partner like Global Village Publishers can help — we routinely digitise bound material as part of our book production process.

### What about old formats?

Slides? Negatives? Audio cassettes? VHS tapes? These require specialised equipment. Do not try to do it all yourself. Professional digitisation services exist in Nairobi, and the investment is worth it for irreplaceable material.

### Follow the 3-2-1 rule

The golden rule of digital preservation:
- **3** copies of your data
- Stored on **2** different types of media
- With **1** copy stored in a separate location

For example: one copy on your office computer, one on an external hard drive kept in a safe, and one in cloud storage (Google Drive, Dropbox, or a dedicated service).

## Step 5: Interview your elders

Some of your most valuable archival material does not exist on paper. It lives in people's heads.

Current and former staff members, alumni, veteran staff who have been with the institution for decades, board members, and community elders carry stories that no document can capture. And every year, more of that oral history is lost.

Start a simple oral history programme:

1. **Identify who to interview**: Start with the oldest living former principals or CEOs, then veteran staff, then notable alumni.
2. **Prepare questions**: Ask about their earliest memories of the institution, major events they witnessed, challenges and triumphs, memorable characters, and how things have changed.
3. **Record everything**: Use your phone or a simple audio recorder. Get consent first. Transcribe key interviews.
4. **Save the recordings**: These become primary sources for future historians — and gold for any commemorative book project.

The stories people tell in interviews are often the most vivid, emotional, and compelling material in a commemorative book. They bring life to the dates and facts.

## Step 6: Make preservation an ongoing habit

Archives are not a one-time project. They are an ongoing discipline.

Designate someone — even if it is part-time — as the institution's archivist or record-keeper. Create a simple policy: what gets kept, where it goes, who is responsible. Make it part of the departure process for staff to surrender any institutional materials they hold.

Schedule regular check-ins. Every quarter, spend an hour filing the past three months' worth of photos and documents. It is far easier to maintain an archive than to rebuild one from scratch.

## How a commemorative book project helps

Here is what we have learned from working with institutions across Kenya: the decision to create a commemorative book is often the catalyst that finally gets the archives in order.

When a school decides to publish a centenary book, suddenly there is a deadline. A reason to dig through those boxes. A budget for professional scanning and organisation. A team assigned to pull everything together.

The book becomes both the *motivation* and the *outcome* of archival preservation. The materials gathered for the project form the foundation of a lasting archive. And the finished book — professionally designed, beautifully printed, case bound — becomes the cornerstone of that archive for future generations.

We have seen institutions discover photographs they did not know existed, reconnect with alumni they had lost touch with, and document histories that were fading from memory — all because they decided to make a book.

## Start today

The single most important step is the first one. Do not wait until your milestone is six months away. Start today.

Take one box, one filing cabinet, one collection, and sort it. Scan ten photos. Write down the names of three people you should interview.

Your institution's story matters. It deserves to be preserved. And with a little effort and the right partners, it can be — not just for this generation, but for the ones that follow.
