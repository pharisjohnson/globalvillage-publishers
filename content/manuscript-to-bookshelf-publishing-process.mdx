# From Manuscript to Bookshelf: The Publishing Process Explained

So you have decided to publish a book. Congratulations that is the most important step.

Now comes the part that catches most people by surprise: the process between "we want a book" and "here is your book" is longer, more detailed, and more collaborative than most expect.

And that is a good thing.

Rushing a book is the fastest way to produce something you will be embarrassed by later. A proper publishing process that produces a book worthy of your institution's legacy takes time, patience, and expertise at every stage.

This guide walks you through each step, from manuscript to bookshelf. We will cover what happens, how long it takes, and what you need to do at each stage. Whether you are a school planning a centenary book, a company documenting your history, or an individual with a story to tell, knowing the process upfront will save you frustration and deliver a better result.

## Phase 1: Manuscript assessment (Weeks 1–3)

Before any editing or design work begins, your manuscript needs a thorough assessment. Think of this as a doctor's checkup for your book.

### What happens

Your manuscript whether it is a rough collection of chapters, a stack of interview transcripts, or a folder of photographs and captions is reviewed by a professional editor. They evaluate:

- **Completeness**: Is anything missing? Are there obvious gaps in the story?
- **Structure**: Does the material flow logically? Is it organised in a way that readers will follow?
- **Tone**: Is the voice consistent? Does it match the purpose of the book?
- **Content quality**: Are there factual issues, contradictions, or weak sections?
- **Photographic coverage**: For commemorative books, are there enough quality images to tell the story visually?

### What you need to do

Provide everything you have even the messy bits. It is better to submit too much material than too little. An editor can help you cut and organise, but they cannot create content out of thin air. If there are gaps in your archive or story, this is the stage where you will identify them, giving you time to fill them in.

### Timeline

1–3 weeks, depending on the manuscript length and complexity.

## Phase 2: Editing (Weeks 4–10)

Editing is where a good manuscript becomes a great one. Most people who have not worked in publishing do not realise there are actually three distinct rounds of editing.

### Developmental editing (Weeks 4–7)

This is the structural edit. Your editor looks at the manuscript as a whole and asks: does this work structurally? Are the chapters in the right order? Are there sections that drag? Is the narrative arc satisfying?

For commemorative and institutional books, developmental editing often involves reordering historical events, strengthening thematic threads, and ensuring the book tells a coherent story rather than a disconnected series of facts.

Developmental editing frequently involves significant rewriting. Whole sections may be cut, expanded, or moved. This can be uncomfortable for authors who are attached to everything they have written but it is essential for creating a book that readers will actually enjoy.

### Copy editing (Weeks 7–9)

Once the structure is locked, your copy editor goes through every line. They correct grammar, spelling, and punctuation. They check for consistency if you spell "organisation" with an "s" on page 5, it should match on page 105. They flag factual inconsistencies, unclear phrasing, and potential legal issues.

Copy editing is meticulous work. A good copy editor catches things everyone else missed including the author, the developmental editor, and everyone else who has looked at the manuscript.

### Proofreading (Week 10)

This is the final polish. After the manuscript has been designed and laid out (see Phase 4), a proofreader reads the formatted pages. They catch any remaining typos, formatting glitches, and errors introduced during the design process.

Note: Proofreading is *not* another round of editing. By this stage, no significant changes should be happening. The proofreader is looking for mistakes, not rewriting text.

### What you need to do

Be available for questions. Editors will need to clarify facts, confirm decisions, and get your approval at each stage. The more responsive you are, the smoother this phase goes.

### Timeline

6–8 weeks total for all three editing rounds.

## Phase 3: Design and typesetting (Weeks 11–16)

While editing is underway, the design team is working on the book's visual identity. But the actual layout called *typesetting* happens once the text is finalised.

### What happens

Your designer takes the edited manuscript and the approved design concept and arranges everything page by page. This includes:

- Placing text within the page grid
- Integrating photographs and illustrations
- Adding captions, pull quotes, and other design elements
- Creating the table of contents
- Designing chapter openers
- Building the cover (front, spine, and back)

Typesetting is where you see your book start to look like a real book for the first time. Designers will produce a set of sample spreads (usually the first few chapters) for your approval before proceeding with the full layout.

### What you need to do

Review sample spreads carefully. This is your last chance to make significant input on layout and visual flow. Once full typesetting begins, major changes become expensive and time-consuming.

Provide final, approved versions of all images. If photographs need colour correction or retouching, this is the time.

### Timeline

5–7 weeks for a typical 100–200 page commemorative book with moderate image content.

## Phase 4: Proofing and approval (Weeks 17–18)

Now you receive a complete, printed proof of your book.

### What happens

We print a physical or digital proof or both showing exactly how the finished book will look. Every page, every image, every line of text is there, formatted and laid out.

You review the proof carefully. Check for:

- Typos, spelling mistakes, and grammatical errors
- Image placement and quality
- Captions matching their photographs
- Page numbers and running heads
- Consistency of headings and subheadings
- Any design issues that bother you

### What you need to do

Read the entire proof. Yes, the whole thing. This is your last chance to catch mistakes. Have multiple people review it fresh eyes always spot things the author misses.

### Timeline

2 weeks for review. Most mistakes are caught in the first week, but we recommend taking the full two weeks.

## Phase 5: Printing (Weeks 19–22)

With final approval given, it is production time.

### What happens

The approved files are sent to the printer. Depending on the complexity and quantity:

- **Digital printing**: Best for short runs (under 500 copies). Faster turnaround, higher per-unit cost.
- **Offset printing**: Best for longer runs (500+ copies). Higher setup cost, lower per-unit cost, superior colour quality.

For premium commemorative books, we typically use offset printing for runs of 500–2000 copies. This gives the best balance of quality and cost.

The printing process includes:
- **Prepress**: Final file preparation, colour proofing, plate making
- **Printing**: The actual press run for each colour
- **Binding**: Folding, gathering, sewing or gluing, casing in
- **Quality control**: Every book is inspected for defects

### What you need to do

Nothing, except wait patiently. If you have done your work in the earlier phases, the printing phase runs smoothly.

### Timeline

3–5 weeks, depending on the printer, binding complexity, and quantity.

## Phase 6: Distribution and delivery (Week 23+)

The books have arrived. Now what?

### What happens

Your books are delivered to your preferred location your school, office, or an event venue. If you need distribution to bookshops, that is a separate arrangement that needs to be planned well in advance.

### What you need to plan for

- **Launch event**: Many institutions hold a book launch tied to their anniversary or milestone. This is a powerful branding and fundraising opportunity.
- **Sales and pricing**: Who will sell the books? At what price? Will proceeds go to a specific fund?
- **Gifting**: Most commemorative books are given to key stakeholders board members, major donors, alumni, partners.
- **Archives**: Keep a few copies in a safe place for your institution's archives.

### Timeline

The first few weeks after delivery are your busiest. Plan ahead for launch logistics, mailing copies to key people, and handling orders.

## The full picture: What timeline should you expect?

For a typical commemorative book 120–180 pages, full colour, hardcover, 500 copies here is a realistic timeline from start to finish:

| Phase | Duration |
|---|---|
| Manuscript assessment | 1–3 weeks |
| Developmental editing | 3–4 weeks |
| Copy editing | 2–3 weeks |
| Design and typesetting | 5–7 weeks |
| Proofing and approval | 2 weeks |
| Printing and binding | 3–5 weeks |
| **Total** | **16–24 weeks** |

That is 4 to 6 months from start to finish. For more complex projects larger books, extensive archival research, multiple interviews add 4–8 weeks.

Is this faster or slower than you expected? Most people are surprised it takes this long. But for a book that will represent your institution for decades, a few months of careful production is a small investment.

Of course, before any of this can happen, your source material needs to be in order. If you are still gathering photographs, documents, and oral histories, our guide to preserving institutional archives will help you prepare ideally before you set a publication deadline.

## The shortcut that is not

We have seen it many times: a client approaches us six weeks before their anniversary with a desperate request to "just throw something together." The result is almost always disappointing. Bad printing. Ugly layout. Typos in the first paragraph. A book that does no justice to the institution's story.

The shortcut is tempting, but it never pays off. A book that matters one you will be proud to give to alumni, board members, and future generations deserves the full process.

Start early. Plan well. Work with professionals who know what they are doing. And trust the process.

Your story is worth it.
