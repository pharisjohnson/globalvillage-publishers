# A Guide to Book Design and Layout for Non-Designers

So you're commissioning a book. You have the content — the photos, the stories, the history. Now it needs to actually *look* like a book. A good one, not just any book.

For most people, the design phase of publishing is a mystery. You know what you like when you see it, but you don't know *why* it works or how to get there. And when a publisher starts talking about "perfect binding," "foil stamping," or "kerning," it's easy to nod along while feeling completely lost.

This guide is for you. No jargon for jargon's sake. Just a clear, practical look at the design and layout decisions that separate an ordinary book from an exceptional one.

## Why design matters more than you think

Here's a truth that surprises many first-time authors: people *do* judge a book by its cover. And by its paper, and its layout, and its binding.

A poorly designed book — no matter how good the content — sends a message of carelessness. It tells readers that the project was rushed, that corners were cut. For a commemorative book that's meant to celebrate an institution's legacy, that's the last message you want to send.

Conversely, a beautifully designed book elevates the content inside. It gives the material weight, authority, and emotional impact. The design isn't decoration — it's communication.

At Global Village Publishers, we've produced hundreds of premium books for Kenyan institutions and individuals. And every single time, the design is where a good project becomes a great one.

## Typography: the invisible art

Typography is the art of arranging type to make language readable and beautiful. It's the foundation of any book, yet most non-designers never think about it.

### Why fonts matter

Every font has a personality. A serif font like Garamond or Baskerville feels traditional, authoritative, and warm — perfect for a school's centenary history. A sans-serif font like Helvetica or Open Sans feels modern, clean, and approachable — great for a contemporary corporate story.

The golden rule: use no more than two or three fonts in a book. One for headings, one for body text, and possibly one for special elements like captions or pull quotes. Mixing too many fonts looks chaotic, like a wardrobe with no colour coordination.

### Size and spacing

Body text should typically be between 10 and 12 points for a coffee table book. But point size alone doesn't determine readability. Line spacing, or *leading*, matters just as much. Too tight and the text feels cramped. Too loose and it feels disconnected.

Margins are another invisible hero. Generous margins give the page room to breathe. They make the book feel luxurious rather than cramped. In premium books, we often use wider margins than you'd see in a novel or textbook — partly for aesthetics, partly because large-format photography needs framing.

## Paper: the feel of the page

Paper choice is one of the most tactile decisions you'll make. In a digital world, the feel of paper is a luxury.

### Coated vs uncoated

Coated paper (glossy or matte) has a smooth finish that makes photographs pop. Colours are more vibrant, details are sharper. This is the standard choice for coffee table books and commemorative volumes because it handles full-colour photography beautifully.

Uncoated paper feels more natural and textured. It's excellent for text-heavy books and gives a more traditional, literary feel. It absorbs ink differently, so colours appear softer and more muted.

### Weight matters

Paper weight is measured in grams per square metre (gsm). For a premium book:
- **Inside pages**: 150–200 gsm is typical for a coffee table book. Light enough to bind well, heavy enough to feel substantial.
- **Cover**: Much heavier — often 250–350 gsm for softcover, or mounted on board for hardcover.

### Kenyan context

In Kenya, we're also mindful of environmental conditions. The right paper choice accounts for humidity and handling. A well-chosen paper stock will hold up beautifully for decades in Kenyan homes and offices.

## Binding: how the book stays together

Binding isn't just functional — it affects how the book opens, how long it lasts, and how premium it feels.

### Saddle-stitch

The simplest binding: folded sheets stapled along the spine. Think magazines and thin booklets. It's economical but not suitable for books over about 48 pages.

**Best for**: Small brochures, event programmes, thin magazines like our *Teachers' Image* periodicals.

### Perfect bound

Pages are glued to the spine with a strong adhesive, then the cover is wrapped around. This is how most paperbacks and many magazines are produced. It handles up to several hundred pages and gives a clean, professional finish.

**Best for**: Softcover books, annual reports, thicker magazines.

### Case bound (hardcover)

The gold standard for premium books. Pages are sewn in signatures (groups of pages), then attached to a hard cover made of board wrapped in cloth, leather, or paper. The book lies flat when open, pages are securely held, and the whole thing feels solid and enduring.

**Best for**: Coffee table books, commemorative volumes, centenary publications — anything meant to last generations.

### Why it matters for Kenyan institutions

When a school commissions a book for its 100-year anniversary, that book needs to survive decades of handling by students, alumni, and visitors. Case binding isn't an extravagance — it's the only choice for that kind of longevity. We guide our clients through this decision based on how the book will be used, how many copies are needed, and what budget is available.

## Cover finishes: the first impression

Your cover has about three seconds to make an impression. These finishing options help it land.

### Matte vs gloss lamination

**Matte**: Subtle, sophisticated, non-reflective. Shows fingerprints less. Feels smooth and modern. Excellent for photography-heavy books.

**Gloss**: Shiny, vibrant, eye-catching. Colours pop more. Reflects light, which can be either dramatic or distracting depending on the setting.

Our recommendation: matte is almost always the right choice for premium commemorative books. It looks more refined and photographs better. Gloss can work for magazines and certain design-forward projects.

### Foil stamping

Metallic foil — gold, silver, copper, or custom colours — applied to the cover. It catches light and creates a premium, celebratory feel. For a centenary book or a corporate milestone, foil stamping on the cover or spine signals that this is something special.

### Embossing and debossing

Embossing raises parts of the cover design, debossing presses them in. Combined with foil stamping, it adds a tactile dimension that makes a book feel truly luxurious. Running your fingers over an embossed title on a hardcover is a small but powerful sensory pleasure.

### Spot UV

A glossy coating applied to specific areas of a matte cover — on the title text, for example, or on a logo. The contrast between matte and glossy in the same surface is striking and modern.

## Layout: guiding the reader's eye

Layout is about flow. Where does the eye go first on each page? How do images and text work together?

### The grid

Professional book designers work with an invisible grid — a system of columns, margins, and alignments that gives every page consistency. Without a grid, pages feel chaotic even if you can't say exactly why.

### Image placement

In a coffee table book, images are the stars. They should be large, well-placed, and properly captioned. Full-bleed images (running to the edge of the page) create impact. Smaller images grouped together tell a story through sequence.

### White space

Don't be afraid of empty space. White space (or negative space) gives the eye a rest. It makes the content that *is* there feel more important. Many first-time book creators try to fill every inch of every page. The best books leave room to breathe.

## The professional difference

Here's the honest truth: you can learn the terminology from a guide like this, but professional book design takes years of practice. The difference between a book designed by someone who knows what they're doing and one designed by a well-meaning amateur is night and day.

A professional designer knows:
- How to choose typefaces that complement the content and each other
- How to balance images and text across a spread
- How to handle colour correction so photos look true to life
- How to prepare files for print to avoid costly mistakes
- How to create a layout that works beautifully from page 1 to page 200

This is why at Global Village Publishers, design is not an afterthought — it's woven into everything we do. From the initial consultation through to the final print approval, our design team works alongside our clients to create books that look as good as the stories they tell.

## Bringing it all together

The best advice we can give any non-designer commissioning a book is this: trust the process, ask questions, and don't settle.

You don't need to become a design expert. You just need to work with people who are. Ask to see samples. Request mockups of key spreads. Get a physical proof before printing. And remember every decision — paper, binding, cover finish, font — adds up to create the reader's experience.

A premium book is an investment. Good design ensures that investment pays off for decades to come.
